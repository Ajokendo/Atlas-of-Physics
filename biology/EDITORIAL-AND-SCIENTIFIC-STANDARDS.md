# Biology Atlas — Editorial and Scientific Quality Standard

**Document type:** project-wide editorial standard for the Biology subject area  
**Languages:** Russian and English  
**Applies to:** chapter text, tables, species profiles, diagrams, interactive models, quizzes, captions and future additions.

## 1. Scientific accuracy and evidence

1. Distinguish established scientific consensus, active research questions, hypotheses and illustrative simplifications. Do not present a model or analogy as a literal description of nature.
2. Support numerical claims with a traceable source and, where relevant, the measurement date, geographic scope, units and method. Avoid false precision.
3. Prefer primary research, peer-reviewed syntheses, official datasets and authoritative scientific institutions. Use news reports only as leads or dated records of a specific event, not as substitutes for population assessments or taxonomic inventories.
4. Never invent references, DOI identifiers, species records, localities, coordinates, population totals or conservation categories. If evidence is incomplete, state the limitation explicitly.
5. For changing information, record the date checked. This is especially important for taxonomy, threatened-species assessments, protected-area boundaries, monitoring results and national statistics.
6. Keep correlation, causation and mechanism distinct. Explain uncertainty and alternative explanations where they materially affect interpretation.

## 2. Taxonomy and biodiversity records

1. Use accepted scientific names consistently and italicize genus and species names. Capitalize the genus; lowercase the specific epithet.
2. When a taxon has recently changed, retain a synonym only when it helps readers connect older literature with the current name. Identify the taxonomic authority or database consulted.
3. Do not infer that a species occurs throughout an oblast or protected area from a country-level range map. Distinguish national range, regional records, verified locality records and direct field observations.
4. Every occurrence claim should ideally identify its source, observation or publication date, locality precision and evidence type (voucher specimen, photograph, camera trap, acoustic record, eDNA, published survey or other documented method).
5. Treat absence of records as lack of evidence, not proof of absence. Explain survey effort and detection limitations when relevant.
6. Keep national Red Data Book status separate from IUCN global or regional categories. Record assessment year and assessment scope; do not transfer one system's category into another.
7. Do not publish sensitive coordinates for threatened species or vulnerable breeding sites where disclosure could increase disturbance, collection or poaching risk.

## 3. Quantitative data and visualizations

1. Every table and chart must identify variables, units, time period, geographic scope and source.
2. Label estimates, measured values, model outputs and illustrative values differently. Never mix them without clear explanation.
3. For percentages, state the denominator. For rates, state the time interval. For population figures, state the survey area and method where available.
4. Maps must include a scale or scale statement, orientation where useful, legend, data date, source and a note about boundary or positional uncertainty.
5. Diagrams must distinguish schematic relationships from scale-accurate structures. Use arrows consistently and explain what they represent.
6. Avoid decorative precision: do not add exact counts, coordinates or scale bars to an illustration unless they are supported by data.

## 4. Interactive models and learning activities

1. State the model's assumptions and limits near the interaction. A qualitative model must not imply quantitative predictive power.
2. Ensure that each control has a meaningful label, visible state and understandable outcome. Keyboard operation and narrow-screen use are required.
3. Use feedback that explains the scientific reason for a correct answer. Distractors should test a real misconception, not rely on ambiguous wording.
4. Do not use a single simplified example to imply that all members of a biological group behave identically. Flag important exceptions.
5. Keep learning objectives, chapter explanations, diagrams, glossary terms and assessment questions aligned.

## 5. Russian–English editorial parity

1. Russian and English versions must communicate the same scientific claims, qualifications, dates, units and learning outcomes.
2. Do not translate scientific names. Use accepted English and Russian common names where stable; otherwise retain the scientific name and explain it.
3. Maintain a shared bilingual glossary for recurring terms. Prefer one translation per concept unless the distinction is explained.
4. Review both languages independently for grammar, scientific meaning, typography and accessibility. Machine translation alone is not a final editorial review.
5. Use SI units by default. If a non-SI unit is common in a field, provide the SI equivalent.

## 6. Sources, attribution and reuse

1. Cite external scientific sources close to the relevant claim and list complete bibliographic details in the chapter's references.
2. Attribute photographs, maps, datasets, adapted diagrams and third-party illustrations individually. Record creator, source, license and any required modification notice.
3. Mark original project diagrams and AI-assisted illustrations honestly. AI generation does not establish ownership of embedded third-party material or guarantee legal clearance.
4. Do not assume that a website's software license also covers its photographs, datasets or other media.
5. The subject's educational text and original learning materials are intended for free, non-commercial educational use with clear attribution to **Atlas of Biology / Atlas of Knowledge** and a link to the source chapter. This editorial statement does not override third-party licenses, the repository's upstream software license, or rights held by external authors. Each reused third-party item remains subject to its own license and attribution terms.
6. Before publishing a new asset, record its provenance and permitted uses. If provenance cannot be established, replace it or omit it.

## 7. Publication review checklist

A chapter is ready for release only when the following have been checked:

- [ ] Scientific claims are traceable to reliable sources.
- [ ] Time-sensitive facts carry dates and appropriate qualifications.
- [ ] Taxonomy and conservation categories have been checked against current authoritative sources.
- [ ] Regional occurrence statements do not exceed the available evidence.
- [ ] Tables, maps and charts show units, dates, scope and sources.
- [ ] Russian and English content are scientifically equivalent.
- [ ] Interactive models explain assumptions and work with keyboard and mobile layouts.
- [ ] Local links, HTML structure and JavaScript pass automated checks.
- [ ] External assets have documented provenance and license conditions.
- [ ] Chapter navigation, glossary and learning checks are consistent.

## 8. Scope and review status

This standard defines the editorial process; it is not a claim that every existing chapter has already passed every checklist item. Existing chapters should be reviewed against it in a documented, staged quality-assurance pass. Where a source or claim cannot yet be verified, preserve the uncertainty rather than silently converting it into a definitive statement.
